\begin{abstract}
Label-free reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards rewards a language model for agreeing with its own majority vote. It improves reasoning at first but eventually collapses: the self-assessed reward goes to one while accuracy falls, often until the model gives the same answer to every problem. Empirically, the collapse appears only when training uses many problems.
We explain this dataset-size dependence as a collective instability.
In a solvable many-problem model in which problems share parameters, a population in which different problems prefer different answers amplifies any shared perturbation for as long as the population-averaged slope $\langle F'\rangle$ of the single-problem self-reinforcement drift $F$ is positive. This holds while problems are still uncertain about their answers.
Per-problem sharpening, slowed by $1/N$ for $N$ training problems, races against the shared mode, which grows at a rate $\propto\lambda^2$ independent of $N$, where $\lambda$ is the strength of the shared coupling. This yields a critical number of problems $N_c\simeq\ln(1/\delta)/(\lambda^2G)$, where $\delta$ is the noise that seeds the shared mode and $G=\ln[F^\star/F(a)]$ decreases with the initial confidence $a$.
Stochastic simulations of the model confirm the predictions: a sharp transition, $N_c\propto\lambda^{-2}$ up to a logarithmic correction, a single critical amplification $\lambda^2NG$ across couplings, confidence profiles and training variants, a merely logarithmic dependence on batch size, and no collapse for confident initial models.
A kernel version of the theory identifies a second, protective coupling (generalization of sharpening) and a marginal-entropy bonus that stabilizes the uniform collective mode.
In label-free RL of a small Transformer, removing the shared direction prevents collapse entirely, restoring it causes collapse, and collapse grows with training-set size and base-model entropy.
\end{abstract}
